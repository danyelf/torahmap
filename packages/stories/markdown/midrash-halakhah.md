description: Which passages of the Torah feed Midrash, and which feed Halakhah.

<!-- stop: talmud_0 | camera: Torah | overlay: commentary | category: Talmud -->

Different commentators find distinct parts of the Tanakh interesting, each bringing their own agendas. The Talmud, for example, is largely legal discussion.

<!-- stop: talmud_1 | camera: Exodus | overlay: commentary | category: Talmud | verse: Exodus.22.8 -->

A portion of Exodus focuses heavily on compensation for loss and theft, and the Talmud refers to it often.

<!-- stop: midrash | camera: Exodus | overlay: commentary | category: Midrash | verse: Exodus.22.8 -->

In contrast, Midrashic commentary (exegetical storytelling) uses this passage, but not as heavily as others.

<!-- stop: midrash_2 | camera: Exodus | overlay: commentary | category: Midrash | verse: Exodus.24.7 -->

There’s other, nearby midrashic fodder: a few passages later, Moses comes before the people with the Book of the Covenant. They promise (in a different translation) נַעֲשֶׂ֥ה וְנִשְׁמָֽע, “We will act, and we will hear!”

<!-- stop: midrash_3 | camera: Exodus | overlay: commentary | category: Midrash | verse: Exodus.24.7 -->

That ordering — the seeming promise of acting before hearing — intrigued storytellers and sages across generations.

<!-- stop: liturgy | camera: Exodus | overlay: commentary | category: Liturgy -->

While the words of the prayers draw heavily from the Tanakh, the words that make it into the prayerbook are sparser.

<!-- stop: liturgy_1 | camera: Torah | overlay: commentary | category: Liturgy -->

Very little from the Torah makes it into the prayerbooks.

<!-- stop: liturgy_2 | camera: Psalms | overlay: commentary | category: Liturgy -->

Many of the Psalms are carried into, and quoted, in the prayer book.

<!-- stop: halakhah | camera: Psalms | overlay: commentary | category: Halakhah -->

In contrast, the Halakha (legal codes) do not mine Psalms for legal guidance.

<!-- stop: shema_talmud | camera: Deuteronomy.6.4 | zoom: 2 | verse: Deuteronomy.6.4 | overlay: commentary | category: Talmud -->

Is there anywhere they all meet? One passage stands out: the Shema. *Hear, O Israel! The ETERNAL is our God, the ETERNAL alone.* The Talmud cites it heavily,

<!-- stop: shema_midrash | camera: Deuteronomy.6.4 | zoom: 2 | verse: Deuteronomy.6.4 | overlay: commentary | category: Midrash -->

and so does the Midrash,

<!-- stop: shema_halakhah | camera: Deuteronomy.6.4 | zoom: 2 | verse: Deuteronomy.6.4 | overlay: commentary | category: Halakhah -->

and the legal codes,

<!-- stop: shema_liturgy | camera: Deuteronomy.6.4 | zoom: 2 | verse: Deuteronomy.6.4 | overlay: commentary | category: Liturgy -->

and the prayer book, which says it twice a day.

<!-- stop: shema_all | camera: everything | verse: Deuteronomy.6.4 | overlay: commentary | category: total -->

The Shema is at once a belief, a law (“inscribe them on the doorposts of your house”), and a prayer, so every braid of the tradition has a reason to return to it. Each reads its own Torah; here, their Torahs overlap.
