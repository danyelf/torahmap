Table I: Chronological overview

[edit]

This table summarises the chronology of the main tables and serves as a guide to the historical periods mentioned. Much of the Hebrew Bible/Old Testament may have been assembled in the 5th century BCE.[8] The New Testament books were composed largely in the second half of the 1st century CE.[9] The deuterocanonical books fall largely in between.

Period Books
Pre-monarchic
13th century–1000 BCE
  • Late 13th century: Song of the Sea[10]
  • 12th–10th: Psalm 29[11][12]
  • Late 12th–late 11th: Song of Deborah[13] (alternative datings to the monarchic period[14] have been advanced)
  • 11th-10th: Numbers 24[15]
  • 12th–8th: Song of Moses[16][17] (not including the editorializing layer of Deuteronomy 32:44–32:55)[17]
Monarchic
1000–587 BCE
  • Late 8th–early 7th: Amos (initial composition of earliest layer, Amos 2:6–9:10);[18] "First Isaiah" (Isaiah 1–39),[19] Hosea,[20] Micah (1–3).[21]
  • 7th: Nahum (based on its assumption of the fall of Thebes and call for the destruction of Nineveh),[22] Zephaniah (in the reign of Josiah, c. 649–609 BCE),[23] Habakkuk (possibly shortly before the Battle of Carchemish, 605 BCE);[24] first edition of the Deuteronomistic history (Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings) in the reign of Josiah;[25] Deuteronomy 5–26 in the reign of Josiah.[26]
  • Late 7th–early 6th: early variation of the Priestly Blessing (from the oldest surviving Biblical text, the Ketef Hinnom scrolls)[27]
Exilic
586–539 BCE
  • Core of Obadiah around the fall of Jerusalem, 586 BCE[28]
  • Completion of Deuteronomistic history (Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings)[29]
  • Deuteronomy expanded with addition of chapters 1–4 and 29–30 to serve as an introduction to the Deuteronomistic history[26]
  • Jeremiah active in the last decade of the 7th century and first decades of the 6th[30]
  • Ezekiel active in Babylon 592–571 BCE[31]
  • "Second Isaiah" (author of Isaiah 40–55) active in Babylon around mid-century[32]
  • Expansion and reshaping of Hosea, Amos, Micah and Zephaniah[33]
  • Possible early Psalms collection (psalms "of David") ending with psalm 89[33]
Post-exilic (Persian)
538–332 BCE
  • Torah (Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers), with 250 BCE determined to be the last possible date for the final text based on manuscript evidence[34][35]
  • Deuteronomy revised with expansions to chapters 19–25 and addition of chapter 27 and 31–34 to serve as conclusion to the Torah[26]
  • "Third Isaiah" (Isaiah 56–66)[32]
  • Later version (the Masoretic Hebrew version) of Jeremiah[36]
  • Haggai (self-dated to the second year of the Persian king Darius 520 BCE)[37]
  • Zechariah (chapters 1–8 contemporary with Haggai, chapters 9–14 from the 5th century)[38]
  • Malachi (5th century BCE, contemporaneous or immediately prior to the missions of Nehemiah and Ezra)[39]
  • Book of Joel (between 450 and 350 BCE)[40]
  • Chronicles (between 350 and 300 BCE)[41]
  • Origins of Ezra–Nehemiah (may have reached its final form as late as the Ptolemaic period, c. 300–200 BCE)[42]
Post-exilic (Hellenistic)
331–164 BCE
  • Job, Ecclesiastes, Proverbs, and Song of Songs (possibly written in the 4th or even 5th century, but seems to reflect contact with Greek culture)[43]
  • Jonah (Persian or Hellenistic, no later than 2nd century BCE)[44]
  • Most of the individual psalms making up the final third of Psalms[45]
  • Tobit (between 225 and 175 BCE)[46]
  • Book of Sirach (the first quarter of the 2nd century BCE, probably c. 175 BCE)[47]
  • Daniel (between 168 and 164 BCE)[48][49][50]
Maccabean/Hasmonean
164–63 BCE
  • 1 Maccabees (between 146 and 129 BCE)[51]
  • Judith (between 150 and 100 BCE)[52]
  • 2 Maccabees (between 134 and 100 BCE)[53]
  • 3 Maccabees (between 100 BCE and 70 CE)[54]
  • Additions to Daniel and Additions to Esther[citation needed]
  • Wisdom of Solomon (c. late 1st century BCE–mid 1st century CE)[43]
Roman
after 63 BCE
  • 3 Maccabees (between 100 BCE and 70 CE)[54]
  • Wisdom of Solomon (c. late 1st century BCE–1st century CE)[43]
  • 4 Maccabees (probably mid 1st century CE)[55]
  • New Testament (between c. 50–110 CE—see Table IV)
